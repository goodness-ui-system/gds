The strip persists above the section row, which is the test for what belongs in it: a fact that only matters inside one section belongs to that section. Two kinds of fact never run together undivided — what the security is, and what the reader holds of it — separated here by a gap wider than any hairline in the row, with the attribution set once on the group. It names the collection rather than the reader, because “your” is false wherever a person can view a book that is not theirs — which is exactly what the subject control exists to allow — and because one heading serves any number of held facts where a prefix has to be pasted onto each. A bare AUM standing among a fund's own attributes reads as the fund's assets under management, a real published figure and a different number by orders of magnitude from the position being described. A label inherits the meaning of its neighbours. An initialism does not rescue this: BOB reads first as a personal name, and it is the ISO 4217 code for the Bolivian boliviano — a currency code beside a currency amount, in a financial product, sitting in the exact position a currency code occupies. One component, two jobs, told apart by label grammar: nouns naming different content against adjectives naming subsets of one thing. What differs visually is only what the difference in job implies — position, because each row attaches to the object it belongs to; scale, because a section switches the whole pane while a scope re-filters one table, and the two levels should not read as equals; and icons, refused on scopes outright and on sections unless every label in the row clears the three tests below. With icons — permitted only when every label has a universal symbol, none is already assigned elsewhere, and none repeats an icon the rail already uses

Four parts carry the style. The rail is one continuous hairline, not a border per child — a per-item border breaks at every row gap and reads as a stack of unrelated fragments rather than a branch. It descends from the centre of the parent's icon, so the children visibly hang from the row that owns them rather than from the panel edge. And the children take no icons: icons mark the top level only, so a child row can never be mistaken for a parent — the indent and the rail already say where it sits. The active child can be marked two ways, and an application picks one and never mixes them. The rail segment, shown second, replaces a length of the branch with the accent: position stays legible while the fill remains reserved for the top level, so the two depths stay distinguishable at a glance — the quieter option, and the default. The fill, shown third, marks the child the way the top level marks itself: impossible to miss, at the cost of that depth cue, and the right choice where level two is where the reader actually lives rather than a place they pass through. Control treatment — a control that holds a value is boxed, one that opens something is not, and a borderless control states its value by filling

The two halves of a toolbar do different work, and the treatment says which is which rather than decorating either. On the left, controls that hold a value: the field needs its box to show where typing goes, and a toggle needs its outline because an unfilled box is what makes "off" legible. On the right, controls that open something: a door has no state to display at rest, so a container around it describes nothing, and nine contours in one row is ink spent saying nothing nine times. Borderless owes one thing in return — a hover ground, since an unbordered control is a weaker affordance at rest and the hover is what repays it. State on that side arrives as a fill rather than a new border, so "has a value" reads identically across the group. Icon-only qualifies twice: permitted where the symbol is genuinely universal and the control either acts or opens a menu, refused where the control holds a value it must show at rest — such a control wears its label and its value like every neighbour. The line is not icon against label, it is door against dial: a door has nothing to report until it opens, a dial that stays silent is lying. And an action standing among configuration controls takes a separator, on the same rule that separates the lens from the scopes: adjacency implies membership.

The page title — three placements, the application decides

A rail already marks the current page. Whether the pane repeats it is the application's call; what is fixed is that one first-level heading always exists, displayed or visually hidden.

The subject control — a page that is always about somebody names who beside the title, and set inline it costs no height at all
Book of Business · J. WhitfieldXYZ-45332▾

Measured: the head is 34px tall with the control and 34px without it. A title at --text-2xl already carries a 34px line box, and a --text-sm control at normal density is 34px, so the control fits inside space the title was occupying anyway. Stacking the same control on its own line is the version that grows the head — 72px against 34px, a full extra step — so stack it only when a long title leaves no room beside it. The control is ghost rather than boxed: it is the same species as the View button, a control whose resting label is its value. The identifier sets in mono one step quieter, because an identifier means its exact characters — and a typographic en dash must never be substituted into one, since XYZ–45332 is not XYZ-45332 and it breaks copy, search, and support tickets.

Placement — adjacent works for a short subject; a long one makes the whole line read as an oversized heading
Book of Business K. Vandermolen-RuizXYZ-45332▾

Right-aligned is the default. Type scale governs rank; distance governs whether two things read as one thing — and setting the subject one step down in a control is not enough on its own, because beside the title it still parses as one long phrase. Pushed to the far end of the row, the title sits alone and reads as a title at any name length, while the subject grows leftward into space that was empty anyway. Two consequences follow: the top-right of that row now belongs to the subject, so the account cluster either moves to the rail's foot or the page head takes its own row beneath the shell bar — what must not happen is subject and account adjacent in one corner without a separator, which is the original ambiguity restated in a new place. And the name caps with an ellipsis as a backstop, full name in the tooltip and in the menu; abbreviating the given name buys roughly forty percent of the length back before truncation ever bites.

Viewing a subject that is not you — marked, and named in words
Book of Business · J. WhitfieldXYZ-45332▾

Read-only viewing earns the marked state and nothing more. Writing as another subject is a different intensity: a persistent banner that survives every navigation, saying whose data will change, with the exit inside it. That one is sized not to how often it happens but to what it costs the once nobody noticed.
